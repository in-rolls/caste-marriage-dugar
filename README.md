

# Caste and Income in Matrimonial Responses

[Read the replication paper](ms/paper.pdf): author claims, research design, contact identification, and income compensation.
A simple model shows how different caste valuations and acceptance beliefs can produce
identical letters. The empirical analysis distinguishes own-caste concentration from rank,
examines both directions of cross-caste contact, and assesses Table 6 and respondent heterogeneity. The appendix reproduces
Tables 4–6 and Figures 1–3. Run `make paper` to rebuild it
from the single [R Markdown source](ms/paper.Rmd) and [validated bibliography](ms/references.bib).

The independent extension preserves the existing PCA and forms attribute thirds within caste.
At Rs 35,000, lower-third Brahmin respondents send 28 letters to the LC ad
and 8 to the Brahmin ad; upper-third respondents send 9 and
76. The lower-third reversal appears in both editions, without education in the
index, and among complete cases. Its same-income crossing is interpolated at
Rs 19,000; the upper groups have no crossing within the tested range.
This is an independent extension, not an exact reconstruction of the independent review’s index or a
willingness-to-pay estimate. Counts, assignments, missingness checks, and 2,000 bootstrap
draws are generated by [src/heterogeneity.R](src/heterogeneity.R).

An R replication and analysis of Dugar, Bhattacharya and Reiley,
[*Can't Buy Me Love? A Field Experiment Exploring the Trade-off Between Income and Caste-Status in an Indian Matrimonial Market*](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1465-7295.2011.00398.x)
(Economic Inquiry, 2012). The authors placed nine fictitious "brides wanted" advertisements in *Anandabazar
Patrika* in September 2007: a Brahmin, a Kayastha and a Namasudra groom, each advertising Rs 7,000, 15,000 or
35,000 a month. The ads ran twice and drew 1,366 letters from brides' families. The paper analyses 1,123 of
them. Each retained letter is linked to one ad. The file does not record a family ranking all nine ads, its exposure, or its contacts with real advertisers.

**The main counts and regressions reproduce; the preference interpretation requires additional assumptions.**
Higher-income lower-caste profiles attract more higher-caste letters. These first contacts combine
preferences, consideration, expected acceptance, and opportunities elsewhere in the market. Table 6
converts response gaps into income using an extrapolated response curve. The [paper](ms/paper.pdf)
distinguishes that calculation from an individual monetary trade-off.

## Measurements and interpretation

| Comparison | Observed pattern and interpretation |
|---|---|
| Own-caste concentration (§4.1). | Each respondent caste sends the largest aggregate share to own-caste ads. Brahmin and Namasudra letter distributions have similar descriptive sorting indices (sorting index 0.38 and 0.38 on a 0-to-1 scale); Kayasthas less (0.13). |
| Direction of cross-caste contact. | Upward letters occur but decline with groom income in these comparisons. Per ad, Kayastha families send 14.1% of their letters to their own groom and 8.9% to the Brahmin; Namasudra families 19.6% to their own, 10.0% to the Kayastha, 3.8% to the Brahmin. At Rs 35,000 Kayastha families send more letters to the Namasudra groom (15.5%) than to the Brahmin (7.2%). This pattern alone does not distinguish horizontal preferences from anticipated rejection or differential consideration. |
| Income and contact rates (abstract). | Downward letters rise with income: Brahmin families' share to the Namasudra groom goes from 1.0% to 10.7%, an elasticity of 1.43. Upward letters *fall* with income: Namasudra families' share to the Brahmin groom drops from 7.7% to 1.1% (elasticity -1.21). A rich higher-caste groom draws fewer lower-caste letters than a poor one. |
| A Namasudra groom at Rs 35,000 needs about Rs 35,900 more to be treated like a Brahmin groom; "almost double his income" (Table 6, §4.3). | That figure divides a 12.3-point share gap by a slope from three income points and extrapolates to Rs 70,900, above every one of the 1,261 real ads the authors collected (maximum Rs 50,000). Its 95% bootstrap interval is Rs 17,300 to 67,900. A logit gives Rs 12,500; the medium-to-high slope gives Rs 45,400. The paper reports no uncertainty. |
| Compensation comparisons by caste and income (§4.3). | Bootstrap intervals on both contrasts include zero; this does not establish that the differences are small ([table](output/audit_table6_contrasts.csv)). |
| Contact decisions and preference interpretation. | A letter mixes desirability with consideration and expected acceptance. Higher income can attract contact without changing caste preferences. Reply, belief, or consideration data, or additional identifying restrictions, would be needed to separate the channels. |
| 1,123 letters are unique responses; 47 non-unique letters from 22 responders were dropped (§4, fn 12). | The file drops 243 letters under a flag named `repeat`. They cluster in the second edition and go disproportionately to lower-caste, high-income grooms. Keeping them roughly halves Table 6 ([table](output/audit_table6_with_repeat_letters.csv)). |
| Respondent attributes and compensation (§4.4). | Rebuilt per the stated recipe: the first principal component carries 16% of the variance and quartile-level figures range from −35,000 to +357,000 rupees. At a median split the direction holds for Brahmin families and reverses for Kayastha families. |

## Letters per advertisement

The cross-caste share of 47% coexists with own caste being the largest pooled destination for each respondent caste. The experimental menu contained three own-caste ads and six other-caste ads; actual consideration sets are unknown.

| Writer's caste | Brahmin ad | Kayastha ad | Namasudra ad |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brahmin (478 letters) | **19.6%** | 8.1% | 5.6% |
| Kayastha (374) | 8.9% | **14.1%** | 10.3% |
| Namasudra (271) | 3.8% | 10.0% | **19.6%** |

Share of the writer caste's letters per ad, averaged over the three incomes. An own-caste ad draws between
1.4 and 5 times the letters of an other-caste ad. Twice as many ads are other-caste, and two fifths of
cross-caste letters are Kayastha families writing in either direction, so the aggregate cross-caste share is
47% while sorting is strong. Cross-caste letters are observed in both directions. These distributions describe recorded contacts, not an identified preference ordering.

## Income equivalents and identifying assumptions

Table 6 asks how much extra income a lower-caste groom would need to attract the same
share of a respondent caste's letters as an own-caste groom at a fixed income. That is an
aggregate contact target under a specified response curve. It does not automatically
identify an individual or average monetary valuation of caste.

Among the 478 retained Brahmin letters,
86 went to the Brahmin groom at Rs 7,000 and
51 to the LC groom at Rs 35,000. These counts describe
contacts to different advertised prospects, not observed choices between two attainable
partners. Different families making the contacts does not itself prevent preference
estimation, but a model is needed to connect those contacts to preferences. The manuscript
shows that match values and acceptance beliefs can change together while preserving
all contact decisions.

**The real menu and feasible prospects are unobserved.** Real advertisements appeared alongside the
experimental profiles, grouped by caste. The supplied real-ad collection describes the distribution of
advertised profiles, but not which profiles each family considered or expected to accept it. Numerous
own-caste advertisements do not establish that an equally attractive own-caste match was attainable.

**Contact costs do not identify a preference ranking.** The monetary cost of a letter was modest, but
search effort, expectations of rejection, and subsequent negotiations may matter. Counts record which
contacts occurred under those conditions. They do not show that each family contacted every acceptable
prospect. Even equal consideration would not isolate preferences from expected acceptance.

**A preference trade-off and an equilibrium cost are different quantities.** Banerjee et al. estimate
preferences from shortlisting and rankings, then use a matching model to study their equilibrium
consequences. Strong preferences can coexist with small equilibrium costs when sufficiently similar
partners are available within caste. Their result is model-dependent and is not a finding that every
family here could remain within caste at no cost.

**Equal contact is not equal match value.** The paper's response curve predicts an income
at which the LC groom would attract as many Brahmin letters as the Brahmin groom. The
high-income comparison extrapolates beyond the tested range. Giving that crossing a
preference interpretation additionally requires restrictions on consideration, acceptance,
and the contact decision. Banerjee et al.'s shortlisting and ranking data supply additional
information for a preference model; their estimates still depend on that model.

What the counts support: how many families of each caste found each ad worth a letter, and how that number
moves with the groom's advertised income. Those are the per-ad shares and the elasticities above.

## What the design identifies

The experiment specifies advertised groom profiles and observes first letters. The observed quantities are the distribution of letters across nine profiles and its association with advertised attributes, for each writer caste. Interpreting these as causal attribute effects requires attention to profile assignment, bundled education, and placement. It does not identify a preference separate from an expectation of acceptance, a price of status
separate from an ad's wording or page position (one ad per cell, run twice), a trade-off within any family
(the nine ads were a few of hundreds a family could write to), or the equilibrium cost of caste barriers in completed marriages.
Letters are concentrated within caste. Income associations differ by the direction of cross-caste
contact, and the highest-income compensation comparison extrapolates beyond the advertised range.

Banerjee, Duflo, Ghatak and Lafortune (2013), reproduced in the sibling repository
[`caste-marriage-banerjee`](https://github.com/in-rolls/caste-marriage-banerjee), watch the other side of the same
newspaper's market five years earlier: real advertisers choosing which letters to pursue. They conclude that
caste preference is for one's own caste, not for higher castes, which is why they argue it costs little to
indulge. The two studies observe different stages and different samples. They cannot simply be joined to identify preferences and acceptance beliefs without assumptions linking populations, periods, and decision rules.

## Reproduce

```sh
make deps     # renv restore into .R/library
make run      # prepare, replicate, compare, audit, figures, README
make test     # gates: row conservation, Tables 4-6 match
make lint
```

R 4.6.0. Original files are untouched in `data/original/` (hashes in `manifest.csv`). The two paper drafts
used are in `sources/` with a [version manifest](sources/paper_versions.csv); the target is the December 2010
final draft, since the journal text is paywalled. Scripts are in `src/`, full-precision CSV tables in `output/`, figures in
`figs/`, and the letter-level codebook in `data/derived/codebook.md`.

The public build uses the numerical [Table 6 worksheet extract](sources/table6_worksheet_values.csv)
and its [source record and calculation notes](sources/table6_worksheet.csv). The original
workbook and private review materials in `oliver/` are not distributed or required to run
the analysis. Superseded working notes remain local in the ignored `notes/` directory.
The public manuscript is maintained in `ms/paper.Rmd`; its tables and inline estimates
come from the CSV outputs. Markdown table copies are not generated.
The bootstrap draw files are generated locally by `make run`; their summary estimates
and intervals are versioned, while the draw files are ignored.
